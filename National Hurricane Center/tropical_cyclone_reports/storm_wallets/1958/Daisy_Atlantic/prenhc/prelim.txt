                  HURRICANE TRACKING WITH RADAR

With the development of weather search radar in recent years, and its
increased use in detecting and tracking hurricanes, a valuable aid has been
made available to the Hurricane Warning Service.

Radar, which can see the characteristic bands of a hurricane up to 200 miles
away makes it possible to locate the eye, or center, of the hurricane while it
is a considerable distance out to sea.  By continued careful observation the
radar meteorologist can determine the speed and direction of past movement,
extent of area of heavy rainfall, and other meteorological factors
characteristic of the hurricane under observation.  The view of the hurricane
obtained by the use of radar combined with observations from other sources
assists the hurricane forecaster in determining the direction the storm will
move and the areas to be warned.

Hurricane Daisy, as it moved northward in August of 1958, provides an
excellent example of hurricane tracking with radar.  As can be seen from the
chart an reverse side, the location of the storm center was reported
frequently and regularly to the forecaster after it came within radar range.
This information, properly evaluated and combined with other data, helped the
forecasters to predict correctly the course of Daisy away from the New England
coast.

Radar fixes, or locations of the center, in themselves cannot use as an
indication of the future movement of the hurricane. As shown on the several
times Daisy appeared for short periods to be moving directly toward land, but
subsequently the apparent direction of motion of the storm became more nearly
parallel to the coast.  This illustrates the sometimes erratic pattern of
movement of the center within the hurricane and the danger of assuming that
the whole storm is moving on the course represented by the last two or three
half-hourly radar fixes.

Occasionally the center fixes reported by different radar stations at about
the same times do not coincide.  Differences in the fixes may be due to
differences in estimates made by the radar observers of the geometric center
of an irregularly shaped eye on the radarscope, difficulties in identifying
the center, or other reasons.  These differences in fixes increase the problem
of interpretation and illustrate further the importance of careful evaluation
of all available data in determining hurricane movement.  Radar center fixes,
properly used, are of great value in hurricane tracking and prediction. 
Failure to take into account their limitations, on the other hand, can result
in serious errors and unnecessary public alarm.

             PRELIMINARY REPORT ON HURRICANE DAISY
                       AUGUST 24-29, 1958


Hurricane "Daisy", intense but small in area, was detected as a tropical storm
about 300 miles east-northeast of Miami, Florida on Sunday, August 24, 1958. 
A report from the ship "Alamar" indicating low pressure and strong winds
provided the first clue to the developing storm.  A Navy reconnaissance flight
into the area subsequently confirmed its existence.

The tropical storm developed into a hurricane on August 25.  From August 24 to
August 27 it drifted very slowly northward.  Then its forward speed up the
coast increased.  It skirted the Atlantic seaboard and finally moved out into
the Atlantic off Nova Scotia on August 29.  Hurricane "Daisy" reached its
maximum intensity late Wednesday, August 27 and early Thursday, August 28
while moving northward off the Georgia and Carolina coasts.  The strongest
winds near the center were then about 125 miles per hour and the central
pressure about 935 mb or 27.61 inches.

Although the center of Hurricane Daisy passed within about 75 miles of Cape
Hatteras and Nantucket, the area of hurricane winds was so concentrated that
neither the North Carolina nor New England coasts felt the full effects of the
storm.  The Hatteras station reported as its highest wind NNW 27 mph with
gusts to 36.  Block Island, R. I. reported a "fastest miles" of 40 mph with a
gust to 45.  A Texas tower located 120 miles east of Cape Cod reported gusty
winds of 87 mph and steady wind of 69 mph as the hurricane center passed
nearby.  Available post-storm reports show no appreciable damage or loss from
this severe hurricane.

Over much of its course along the coast, Daisy was in range of shore-based
radar.  From the time it approached Hatteras until it passed Nantucket, the
center was under almost constant radar observation.  Reconnaissance coverage
by Air Force, Navy, and National Hurricane Research Project aircraft was also
excellent, and the slow movement of the storm near the Florida coast during
the first several days of its existence offered an unusually good opportunity
for investigation by aircraft of the National Hurricane Research Project based
at West Palm Beach.

BOSTON WEATHER BUREAU HURRICANE ADVISORY 22 11 FM EST AUGUST 29 1958.

HURRICANE DAISY HAS RAPIDLY LOST ITS HURRICANE CHARACTERISTICS IN THE PAST 6 HOURS AND AT 11 PM
EST...0400Z...STORM DAISY WAS LOCATED BY RECONNAISSANCE AIRCRAFT AT LATITUDE 43.0 NORTH AND LONGITUDE
62.2 WEST.  THE STORM WAS MOVING EAST NORTHEASTWARD AT 32 MPH.  IT IS EXPECTED TO MOVE IN THE SAME
DIRECTION AT 32 TO 35 MPH IN THE NEXT 12 HOURS.

HIGHEST WINDS IN STORM DAISY ARE NOW ONLY 70 MPH THE CENTER AND GALES EXTEND OUTWARD 250 MILES IN THE
SOUTHERN SEMICIRCLE AND 100 MILES IN THE NORTHERN SEMICIRCLE.  STORM DAISY WILL CONTINUE TO DIMINISH
IN INTENSITY.

THIS IS THE FINAL NUMBERED ADVISORY ON STORM DAISY.  FURTHER INFORMATION ON TM STORM WILL BE
CONTAINED IN THE NSS BULLETINS.

PIERCE WEATHER BUREAU BOSTON
